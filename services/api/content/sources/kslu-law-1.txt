# llb-1-contract-1 | 1 | Law of Contract I: General Principles
## Formation of Contract
### Offer, acceptance and communication
= Proposal, acceptance, communication and revocation under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.
- A proposal (section 2(a)) becomes a promise when accepted (2(b)); an agreement enforceable by law is a contract (2(h)).
- An invitation to treat (display of goods, advertisements, auction calls) is not an offer (Pharmaceutical Society v Boots; Harvey v Facey).
- A general offer can be accepted by anyone performing its conditions (Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.; Lalman Shukla v Gauri Dutt: knowledge of the offer is needed).
- Communication of acceptance is complete against the proposer when posted, and against the acceptor when it reaches the proposer (section 4).
> Distinguish offers from invitations to treat
> Decide when a contract is concluded by post or electronically
hook: A shop tags a TV at ₹1,000 by mistake. Can a customer insist on buying it at that price?
q: Is a price list an offer? || No, it is an invitation to treat.
q: When is acceptance by post complete against the proposer? || When the letter of acceptance is posted, putting it out of the acceptor's power.
terms: proposal; acceptance; invitation to treat; general offer
## Consideration and Capacity
### Consideration and competence to contract
= Consideration, privity, and minors and persons of unsound mind.
- Consideration may move from the promisee or any other person (section 2(d); Chinnaya v Ramayya).
- An agreement without consideration is void except as section 25 allows (natural love and affection in writing and registered, past voluntary service, promise to pay a time-barred debt in writing).
- A minor's agreement is void ab initio (Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose, 1903); no estoppel against a minor.
- Persons of unsound mind cannot contract while unsound (section 12).
> Apply the rules of consideration
> Advise on contracts with minors
## Free Consent
### Coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation and mistake
= Factors vitiating consent and their effect.
- Consent is free unless caused by coercion (15), undue influence (16), fraud (17), misrepresentation (18) or mistake (20–22).
- Coercion, undue influence, fraud and misrepresentation make the contract voidable (sections 19, 19A).
- A bilateral mistake of fact essential to the agreement makes it void (section 20); a mistake of Indian law does not (section 21).
> Identify the factor vitiating consent
> State the effect on the contract
## Void Agreements, Discharge and Remedies
### Void agreements and discharge
= Unlawful objects, restraint of trade, wagers and frustration.
- Agreements in restraint of trade are void (section 27) except sale of goodwill; post-employment restraints are void in India.
- Wagering agreements are void (section 30).
- Frustration: a contract becomes void when performance becomes impossible or unlawful (section 56; Satyabrata Ghose v Mugneeram Bangur).
> Identify void agreements
> Apply the doctrine of frustration
### Breach and remedies
= Damages, quasi-contracts and specific relief.
- Damages for loss arising naturally or within the parties' contemplation (section 73; Hadley v Baxendale); remote losses are not recoverable.
- Liquidated damages and penalties: reasonable compensation up to the stated sum (section 74).
- Quasi-contracts (sections 68–72): e.g. payment by mistake must be repaid (section 72).
- After the 2018 amendment of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, specific performance is the general rule, not discretionary.
> Compute damages for breach
> Advise on specific performance

# llb-1-constitutional-law-1 | 1 | Constitutional Law I
## Nature of the Constitution
### Salient features and the Preamble
= Making of the Constitution, its features and the Preamble.
- The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950.
- Features: written, federal with a strong centre, parliamentary government, fundamental rights, independent judiciary, rigid and flexible amendment.
- The Preamble is part of the Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973); "socialist", "secular" and "integrity" were added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976.

## Fundamental Rights: Equality
### Articles 12–18
= State, judicial review, equality and reservation.
- "State" under Article 12 includes instrumentalities of the State (Ajay Hasia; Pradeep Kumar Biswas).
- Article 14 permits reasonable classification (intelligible differentia with a rational nexus) and forbids arbitrariness (E. P. Royappa).
- Indra Sawhney (1992): 50% ceiling on reservation, creamy layer exclusion; Janhit Abhiyan (2022) upheld the EWS quota.
> Apply the test of reasonable classification
> Explain the law on reservation
## Fundamental Rights: Freedoms and Life
### Articles 19–22
= Freedoms, protections in criminal matters and right to life.
- Article 19(1) freedoms are subject to reasonable restrictions in 19(2)–(6).
- Article 20: no ex post facto criminal law, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination.
- Article 21: procedure must be fair, just and reasonable (Maneka Gandhi, 1978); privacy is a fundamental right (K. S. Puttaswamy, 2017).
- Article 22: an arrested person must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours.
> Apply Article 21 jurisprudence
> Explain safeguards against arrest

## Remedies, DPSP and Duties
### Constitutional remedies, directive principles and duties
= Writs, Article 32 and 226, DPSPs and fundamental duties.
- Article 32 is a fundamental right to move the Supreme Court; High Courts issue writs under Article 226 (wider: "for any other purpose").
- Writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto.
- DPSPs (Part IV) are not enforceable but fundamental in governance; harmony with fundamental rights is a basic feature (Minerva Mills, 1980).
- Fundamental duties (Article 51A) were added by the 42nd Amendment.

# llb-1-torts | 1 | Law of Torts, Motor Vehicles Act and Consumer Protection
## General Principles
### Nature of tort
= Definition, elements and maxims.
- A tort is a civil wrong, other than breach of contract or trust, remedied by unliquidated damages.
- Injuria sine damno is actionable (Ashby v White); damnum sine injuria is not (Gloucester Grammar School case).
- General defences: volenti non fit injuria, inevitable accident, act of God, private defence, necessity, statutory authority.
> Identify the elements of a tort
> Apply general defences
## Specific Torts
### Negligence
= Duty of care, breach, causation and damage.
- Duty of care: neighbour principle (Donoghue v Stevenson, 1932).
- Breach is judged by the reasonable person standard; res ipsa loquitur infers negligence from the facts.
- Medical negligence: the Bolam test as applied in Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab (2005).
> Establish the elements of negligence
> Apply res ipsa loquitur
### Strict and absolute liability, nuisance and defamation
= Rylands v Fletcher, absolute liability and other torts.
- Strict liability (Rylands v Fletcher, 1868) for escape of dangerous things, with exceptions.
- Absolute liability for hazardous industries, with no exceptions (M. C. Mehta v Union of India, 1987, the Oleum gas case).
- Defamation: libel (permanent form) and slander (transient); defences are truth, fair comment and privilege.
> Distinguish strict and absolute liability
> Apply defences to defamation
## Vicarious Liability and the State
### Vicarious liability
= Master and servant, and liability of the State.
- An employer is liable for torts of employees in the course of employment.
- State liability: sovereign functions (Kasturilal, 1965) narrowed by later cases awarding compensation for violation of fundamental rights (Rudul Sah; Nilabati Behera).
- Constitutional tort: compensation under Articles 32 and 226.
> Decide when an employer is vicariously liable
> Explain State liability in India
## Motor Vehicles and Consumer Law
### Motor accident claims and consumer protection
= MV Act claims and the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.
- Claims tribunals (MACT) decide motor accident compensation; no-fault liability is ₹5 lakh for death and ₹2.5 lakh for grievous hurt (section 164).
- Compensation by the multiplier method (Sarla Verma; Pranay Sethi).
- Consumer Protection Act, 2019: consumer rights, product liability, unfair trade practices, e-commerce; district, state and national commissions.

- Sources: Shruti, Smriti, commentaries and digests, custom, legislation and judicial decisions.

## Marriage and Divorce
### Hindu Marriage Act, 1955
= Conditions of marriage, void and voidable marriages, and matrimonial remedies.
- Conditions (section 5): monogamy, sound mind, age 21 (groom) and 18 (bride), not within prohibited degrees or sapindas (unless custom permits).
- Saptapadi completes the marriage where it is the essential ceremony (section 7).
- Divorce grounds (section 13) include cruelty, desertion for two years, conversion and mental disorder; divorce by mutual consent under section 13B (the six-month period may be waived: Amardeep Singh, 2017).

### HAMA and HMGA
= Adoption, maintenance and guardianship under the 1956 Acts.

- Wife, children and aged parents are entitled to maintenance; also BNSS section 144 (formerly CrPC 125) for all communities.
- Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956: welfare of the minor is paramount; the mother can be natural guardian (Githa Hariharan, 1999).

= Coparcenary, intestate succession and the 2005 amendment.
- Since the 2005 amendment, a daughter is a coparcener by birth (section 6); applies regardless of whether the father was alive in 2005 (Vineeta Sharma, 2020).
- Class I heirs (son, daughter, widow, mother and others) take simultaneously and equally.
- A Hindu female's property is her absolute property (section 14).

# llb-1-criminal-law-bns | 1 | Criminal Law I: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023
! Section numbers are of the BNS; the IPC equivalents may still be expected in examinations.
## General Principles
### Crime and its elements
= Actus reus, mens rea and stages of crime.
- Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea: the act is not guilty unless the mind is guilty.
- Stages: intention, preparation, attempt (punishable, section 62), commission.
- Joint liability: common intention (section 3(5)) and common object of an unlawful assembly (section 190).
> Identify actus reus and mens rea
> Distinguish preparation and attempt
## General Exceptions
### General exceptions and private defence
= Mistake, accident, infancy, insanity, intoxication and private defence.
- Nothing is an offence by a child under 7 (section 20); a child of 7–12 lacking maturity of understanding is also exempt (section 21).
- Insanity (section 22) follows the M'Naghten rules: incapacity to know the nature of the act or that it is wrong.
- Private defence (sections 34–44) extends to causing death in cases listed in sections 38 (body) and 41 (property).
> Apply the general exceptions
> Decide the limits of private defence
## Offences against the Body
### Culpable homicide and murder
= Distinguishing culpable homicide from murder and their punishments.
- Culpable homicide (section 100); murder (section 101) with exceptions such as grave and sudden provocation and exceeding private defence.
- Murder is punished under section 103; murder by a group of five or more on grounds such as race, caste or religion (mob lynching) is punishable under section 103(2).
- Reg v Govinda guides the distinction between culpable homicide and murder.
> Distinguish culpable homicide and murder
> Apply the exceptions to murder

terms: culpable homicide; murder; provocation; mob lynching
## Offences against Property and New Offences
### Theft, extortion, robbery, cheating and organised crime
= Property offences and offences new in the BNS.
- Theft (section 303) needs dishonest intention, movable property, without consent, and moving it; snatching is a separate offence (section 304).
- Robbery (section 309) is theft or extortion with violence; dacoity (section 310) is robbery by five or more.
- Cheating (section 318) and criminal breach of trust (section 316).
- New offences: organised crime (section 111), petty organised crime (112), terrorist act (113).
> Distinguish theft, extortion, robbery and dacoity
> Identify the new offences in the BNS

= Contracts of indemnity and guarantee and the surety's rights.

- Guarantee (section 126): three parties — creditor, principal debtor, surety; the surety's liability is co-extensive with the debtor's (section 128).

- The bailee must take the care a person of ordinary prudence takes of his own goods (section 151).
- Pledge (section 172) is bailment as security for a debt; the pawnee may sell after reasonable notice.

### Contract of agency
= Creation, authority and termination of agency.
- An agent is employed to act for another in dealings with third persons (section 182).

# llb-3-jurisprudence | 3 | Jurisprudence | year=2025-26
## Schools of Jurisprudence
### Natural law and positivism
= Natural law and the analytical school.
- Natural law: law derives from reason or morality (Aquinas; modern: Fuller's inner morality of law).
- Austin: law is the command of the sovereign backed by sanction.
- Hart: law is a union of primary rules (duties) and secondary rules (recognition, change, adjudication).
- Kelsen's pure theory: a hierarchy of norms resting on the grundnorm.
> Compare natural law and positivism
> Explain Hart's rule of recognition
### Historical, sociological and realist schools
= Savigny, Pound and legal realism.
- Savigny: law grows from the Volksgeist (spirit of the people).
- Pound: law as social engineering balancing interests.
- Realists (Holmes, Llewellyn): law is what courts actually do.
> Explain the historical school
> Apply Pound's theory of interests
## Sources of Law
### Legislation, precedent and custom
= Sources of law and the doctrine of precedent.
- Precedent: ratio decidendi binds; obiter dicta persuade; Article 141 binds all courts by the Supreme Court's law.
- A valid custom must be ancient, certain, reasonable and continuous.
- Delegated legislation must stay within the parent Act.
> Distinguish ratio and obiter
> Test the validity of a custom
## Legal Concepts
### Rights, duties and legal personality
= Hohfeld's analysis, possession, ownership and persons.
- Hohfeld's jural correlatives: right–duty, privilege–no-right, power–liability, immunity–disability.
- Possession has corpus (control) and animus (intention).
- Legal persons include corporations, idols (Ram Janmabhoomi case) and, by some courts, rivers.
> Apply Hohfeld's scheme
